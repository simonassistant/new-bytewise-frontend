## Part 1: Mastering Academic Reading Skills

> **Purpose of this Section:** University-level English courses require you to engage deeply with complex ideas. In this section, you will learn how to effectively read academic papers – not just for comprehension, but for critical analysis, argument identification, and information extraction. These skills are fundamental for all your university studies, and they are especially crucial for successfully developing your upcoming Position Paper.

### ACADEMIC READING: SKIMMING AND SCANNING SKILLS
In university, you'll encounter a vast amount of reading material. You won't always have time to read every word of every paper. That's where skimming (reading quickly for general understanding) and scanning (searching for specific information) become invaluable skills. They allow you to quickly grasp the main ideas of a text or locate specific information you need, saving you time and helping you work more efficiently. These skills are particularly useful when you're conducting research for your Position Paper, as they help you quickly determine if an article is relevant to your argument.

#### Activity 1.1: Skimming an Empirical Paper
> To practice the skill of skimming, which means reading quickly to get a general understanding of an article's main topic, purpose, and overall structure, without focusing on every detail. Before you commit to reading an entire academic paper, skimming helps you decide if it's even relevant to your research or if it contains the kind of information you're looking for.

**Instructions:**
1.  **Start skimming *Article A* (2 minutes)**
    1.1. **Step 1:** Read the title and abstract. What are the very first clues about the paper's subject?
    1.2. **Step 2:** Read all the main section headings. What are the major parts or stages of the research/ argument?
    1.3. **Step 3:** Read the first and last sentence of each paragraph in the Introduction and Discussion sections.
    1.4. **Step 4:** Look at any Tables or Figures and read their Titles.
2.  **Reflect and summarize**
    2.1. Without looking back at the article, write one clear sentence summarizing what you think *Article A* is about.

> **Activity 1.1 (Answers)**
> 1.1. Parent's attitude on the use of FRT technology in elementary schools in China.
> 1.2. Introduction, Literature Review, Research Model & Hypothesis, Method, Data Analysis, Discussion
> 1.3. (Not provided)

### ACADEMIC READING: LOCATING THE CORE ARGUMENTS
Academic papers are built on arguments. To truly understand and critically engage with an article, you need to be able to identify the author's main argument – their thesis statement – and the individual points they use to support it – their topic sentences.

- **Thesis Statement:** The central argument of the entire paper, usually found towards the end of the Introduction.
- **Topic Sentence:** The main idea of a single body paragraph, usually the first sentence.

#### Activity 1.3: Identifying Thesis Statements and Topic Sentences
> To practice locating the thesis statement and topic sentences within an academic text.

**Instructions:**
1.  Read the Introduction of *Article B* carefully. Identify the thesis statement.
2.  Read the two provided Body Paragraphs from *Article B*. Identify the topic sentence of each paragraph.
3.  Reflect and discuss with a classmate.

> **Activity 1.3 (Answers)**
> **1. Thesis Statement Identification:**
> > The identified thesis statement – "However, in this article, we seek to problematise the specific connotations and possible consequences of facial recognition technology in schools. Drawing on emerging debates amongst communications, media and surveillance scholars, the article addresses a number of specific social challenges and concerns – not least various ways in which this technology might alter the nature of schools and schooling along divisive, authoritarian and oppressive lines." – effectively serves as the article's core argument.
>
> It makes a clear, debatable claim about the negative consequences of facial recognition in schools. It explicitly sets up the rest of the paper by signaling that the following sections will delve into specific challenges and concerns, drawing on scholarly debates to detail how the technology might lead to divisive, authoritarian, and oppressive changes.
>
> **2. Topic Sentence Identification:**
> > **Topic Sentence 1:** "One prominent educational application of facial recognition technology is campus security."
>
> This sentence clearly introduces the paragraph's specific point: the use of facial recognition for campus security. The rest of the paragraph supports this by providing details on its application for security purposes in schools. This connects to the overall thesis by highlighting one of the connotations (safety/security) that the authors will challenge.
>
> > **Topic Sentence 2:** "Another application of facial recognition in schools is attendance monitoring."
>
> This sentence introduces the paragraph's distinct focus on attendance monitoring. The subsequent sentences support this by detailing its popularity and perceived benefits. This provides another concrete example of the technology's connotations (efficiency/convenience) that will be problematized later.
>
> **3. Reflection:**
> Each topic sentence acts as a mini-argument that directly contributes to the paper's overarching thesis. Being able to quickly locate these key sentences allows readers to efficiently grasp the main argument and follow the logical progression of ideas.

### INTRODUCTION TO THE IMRAD STRUCTURE
Most empirical journal articles follow a standard **IMRaD** structure:
-   **I**ntroduction: Background, literature review, research question.
-   **M**ethods: How the study was conducted (participants, materials, procedures).
-   **R**esults: Findings presented with data, tables, and figures.
-   **a**nd
-   **D**iscussion: Interpretation of results, significance, limitations, future research.

#### Activity 2.2: Guided Outline Activity – Mapping the Empirical Argument
> To read a full empirical paper (*Article A*) and create a structured outline to understand how researchers build their arguments.

**Instructions:**
Read *Article A* section by section and fill in the outline template.

> **Activity 2.2 (Answers)**
> **Article Title:** Supporting schools to use face recognition systems: a continuance intention perspective of elementary school parents in China
> **Authors:** Jon-Chao Hong, Yushun Li, Shuo-Ying Kuo, Xin An
>
> **I. Introduction**
> - **a. General problem/context:** Parents' attitudes towards the use of Facial Recognition Systems.
> - **b. Previous research:** The public has concerns about privacy and human rights. Parents worry about privacy violations and data leakage.
> - **c. Research gap:** It is necessary to explore factors influencing parents' intention to use these systems to gain their trust.
> - **d. Research question(s):** To investigate the relationships between parents' DBVW, technological innovativeness, perceived value, and their continuance intention to use the technology.
>
> **II. Methods**
> - **a. Participants:** 394 parents of elementary students from Xuzhou, China (snowball sampling).
> - **b. Instruments:** Online questionnaire adapted from existing literature.
> - **c. Procedure:** The questionnaire was distributed via email to Parent Associations.
>
> **III. Results**
> - **Key Finding(s):** DBVW was negatively correlated with perceived value. Technological innovativeness and perceived value were positively correlated with continuance use intention.
>
> **IV. Discussion**
> - **a. Interpretation:** When parents find a system useful, they are more willing to use it.
> - **b. Hypothesis Support:** The findings supported all initial hypotheses.
> - **c. Limitations:** The study did not examine the usage timing and scope of the FR system.
> - **d. Future research:** Future studies could focus on classroom management applications.

## Part 3: Engaging with a Conceptual Journal Article

> **Purpose of this Section:** Many papers in the humanities are conceptual or theoretical, developing new theories or critiquing ideas without new empirical research. Understanding them is vital for developing your own arguments.

#### Activity 3.1: Reading the Abstract of a Conceptual Paper
> To learn how to read an Abstract to understand the core argument of a conceptual paper, where the focus is on ideas and arguments rather than data.

**Instructions:**
Read the Abstract of *Article B* and answer the following questions.
- **Question 1: Context/Problem:** What problem are the authors addressing?
- **Question 2: Authors' Position/Thesis:** What is the authors' main argument?
- **Question 3: Approach/Roadmap:** How will the authors build their argument?
- **Question 4: Contribution/Conclusion:** Why is this argument important?

> **Activity 3.1 (Answers)**
> **1. Context/Problem:**
> > The authors are addressing the growing, largely unquestioned integration of facial recognition technology into schools, where it's currently viewed as a "routine addition." They highlight a gap in critical discussion about the broader "social challenges and concerns."
>
> **2. Authors' Position/Thesis:**
> > The authors' main argument is that school-based facial recognition technology presents significant social challenges and is likely to alter schooling in ways that are "divisive, authoritarian and oppressive."
>
> **3. Approach/Roadmap:**
> > The authors will build their argument by first establishing the background of the technology's integration, then contending that it poses specific social challenges, and finally considering if any valid justification exists for its use in schools.
>
> **4. Contribution/Conclusion:**
> > This argument is important because it shifts the debate from routine acceptance to critical social implications. Its main contribution is to problematize the technology and initiate a discussion on whether it should be allowed in schools at all.

---

### ACADEMIC READING: FOLLOWING SIGNPOSTS
Conceptual papers have unique, argument-driven structures. Authors provide "signposts" (headings, subheadings, transitional language) to guide readers. Learning to read these signposts helps you follow the logical flow of the argument.

#### Activity 3.2: Following the Argument's Signposts
> To recognize how headings and subheadings create a clear "roadmap" of the writer's argument in a conceptual paper.

**Instructions:**
1.  Recall the "Approach/Roadmap" from the Abstract of *Article B*.
2.  Arrange the scrambled headings below in the most logical order based on the abstract and thesis statement.
    > **Thesis Statement:** However, in this article, we seek to problematise the specific connotations and possible consequences of facial recognition technology in schools. Drawing on emerging debates amongst communications, media and surveillance scholars, the article addresses a number of specific social challenges and concerns – not least various ways in which this technology might alter the nature of schools and schooling along divisive, authoritarian and oppressive lines.
    >
    > **Scrambled Section Headings from Article B:**
    > - Introduction
    > - Challenging the take-up of facial recognition in schools
    > - Discussion
    > - Facial recognition technologies in education
    > - Conclusion
    > - The emergence of facial recognition technology across society
    > - Making sense of the take-up of facial recognition technology in schools
    > - Problematising the rise of facial recognition
3.  Share your order with a partner and explain your reasoning.

> **Activity 3.2 (Answers)**
> **2. Correct Order of Section Headings:**
> - Introduction
> - The emergence of facial recognition technology across society
> - Problematising the rise of facial recognition
> - Facial recognition technologies in education
> - Making sense of the take-up of facial recognition technology in schools
> - Challenging the take-up of facial recognition in schools
> - Discussion
> - Conclusion
>
> **3. Rationale for the Order:**
> The logical flow is driven by the argument. It begins with an **Introduction** to set the context. It then moves from the general (**The emergence of facial recognition technology across society**) to the specific (**Facial recognition technologies in education**). The argument is built progressively: first by establishing the technology's presence, then **Problematising** its rise, analyzing why schools adopt it (**Making sense of the take-up**), and finally presenting the core argument (**Challenging the take-up**). This climactic point directly addresses the "divisive, authoritarian and oppressive" consequences mentioned in the thesis. The paper then logically moves to a **Discussion** to reflect on broader implications and finishes with a **Conclusion** to summarize and offer final thoughts.

#### Activity 3.3: Argument Mapping Activity – Deconstructing the Conceptual Argument
> To read a full conceptual paper (*Article B*) and create an outline that maps the author's central argument and key supporting points.

**Instructions:**
Read *Article B* section by section and fill in the argument map template.

> **Activity 3.3 (Answers)**
> **Article Title:** Facial recognition technology in schools: critical questions and concerns
> **Authors:** Mark Andrejevic & Neil Selwyn
>
> **Overall Thesis Statement:**
> > However, in this article, we seek to problematise the specific connotations and possible consequences of facial recognition technology in schools. Drawing on emerging debates amongst communications, media and surveillance scholars, the article addresses a number of specific social challenges and concerns – not least various ways in which this technology might alter the nature of schools and schooling along divisive, authoritarian and oppressive lines.
>
> **I. The Problem/Question**
> > The authors are addressing the problem that facial recognition technology is being increasingly introduced into schools with little public controversy or critical scrutiny. They challenge this uncritical acceptance by exposing the specific social consequences and ethical concerns related to this technology's presence in educational environments.
>
> **II. Body Sections (Supporting Points):**
> - **Supporting Point 1:**
>   - **Topic Sentence:**
>     > One prominent educational application of facial recognition technology is campus security.
>   - **Key evidence or reasoning used:**
>     - Prevalence in the US due to school shootings.
>     - Significant spending ($2.7 billion annually) on campus security.
>     - Vendor marketing of systems as a protective "all-seeing shield."
>     - Enables authorities to control campus access and track individuals.
>
> - **Supporting Point 2:**
>   - **Topic Sentence:**
>     > Another application of facial recognition in schools is attendance monitoring.
>   - **Key evidence or reasoning used:**
>     - Promises to eliminate inefficiencies of manual roll-calls.
>     - Popular in the UK and Australia.
>     - Specific examples like the "Loop-Learn" system saving teacher time.
>     - Seen as effective for combating "fake attendance" in countries like India.
>
> - **Supporting Point 3:**
>   - **Topic Sentence:**
>     > Facial recognition is also being used in a number of 'virtual learning' contexts.
>   - **Key evidence or reasoning used:**
>     - Ensures the integrity of online courses.
>     - Controls access to online educational content.
>     - Used for "e-assessment security" to verify student identity during online exams.


***

-   **Supporting Point 4:**
    -   **Topic Sentence:**
        > Finally, there is a growing interest in facial detection techniques as an indicator of student 'engagement' and learning.
    -   **Key evidence or reasoning used:**
        -   Research claims that detecting "brief facial actions" can accurately indicate student engagement (or non-engagement) in online learning environments, showing states like boredom, confusion, delight, frustration, etc.
        -   Specific "insightful facial actions" for learning include brow-raising, eyelid tightening, and mouth dimpling.
        -   "Facial microexpression states" (lasting less than half a second) are believed to correlate with conceptual learning and offer insight into learners' thoughts.
        -   The face is viewed as a "continuous and non-intrusive way" to understand a learner's current mental state.
        -   Learning scientists focus on "academic emotions" (e.g., contentment, anxiety), engagement, and "epistemic" emotions, believing detection can highlight learning problems.
        -   Some educationalists anticipate the mass deployment of facial learning detection to identify "productive states" in student groups in real-time.

-   **Supporting Point 5:**
    -   **Topic Sentences:**
        > Schools are thereby positioned as relatively conducive contexts for the introduction of facial recognition technologies. This apparent 'good fit' between schools and facial recognition is supported by a couple of straightforward practicalities. First, is the infrastructural ease with which facial recognition can be implemented and adopted across schools.
    -   **Key evidence or reasoning used:**
        -   The technology "fits neatly" with established school practices and existing infrastructure.
        -   Schools have long-standing traditions of routinely collecting and maintaining photographic records of students' faces.
        -   This allows facial recognition systems to easily appropriate and utilize these existing name-and-face photographic databases.
        -   Schools have relatively stable populations, making practical implementation easier compared to more "open" institutional settings like hospitals or libraries.

-   **Supporting Point 6:**
    -   **Topic Sentence:**
        > Another factor which may well be hastening the implementation of facial recognition systems in schools is their already extensive video monitoring and closed-circuit surveillance infrastructures.
    -   **Key evidence or reasoning used:**
        -   Schools have enthusiastically adopted CCTV over the past 20 years, leading to campus-wide surveillance coverage.
        -   Some schools have already adopted other surveillance technologies like teacher body-cameras, fingerprint enrollment, and RFID-tagging of students.
        -   In the US, school shootings have driven the rise of surveillance-based "solutions," including "smart cameras" from companies like ZeroEyes (claiming to recognize armed attackers) and Athena (claiming to recognize patterns of violent behavior).
        -   There is also a push for video monitoring of classroom teachers to improve instructional practices.

-   **Supporting Point 7:**
    -   **Topic Sentences:**
        > Nevertheless, despite these correspondences, there are a number of ways in which the emerging presence of facial recognition technology in schools might be questioned, if not robustly challenged. To date, any push-back against facial recognition in schools has tended to focus on discomforts around vaguely expressed notions of 'privacy' and/or doubts over the technical efficiency of particular systems.
    -   **Key evidence or reasoning used:**
        -   Existing push-back is often limited to concerns about "privacy" or technical efficiency.
        -   An example is the Swedish 'Datainspektionen' fining a school for a facial recognition roll-call system, citing issues with student consent due to power imbalances.

-   **Supporting Point 8:**
    -   **Topic Sentence:**
        > In terms of academic commentary, questioning to date of facial recognition in education has tended to focus on the pedagogical implications of using facial recognition and other biometric technologies as classroom tools to support learning and teaching assessment.
    -   **Key evidence or reasoning used:**
        -   Saltman (2016) argues that using emotion gauging reduces learning to passive consumption and teaching to merely "capturing student attention," rather than fostering dialogue or experimentation.
        -   Williamson (2017) supports this, highlighting that biometrics act as "persuasive computing" that reframes learning as individual psychological behavior change, reducing it to observable traits that can be manipulated.

-   **Supporting Point 9:**
    -   **Topic Sentences:**
        > [T]he statistical processes through which facial recognition technologies quantify and frame a student's face are inherently reductive. This constitutes a very reductive engagement with students in contrast to how they would ordinarily be viewed by a human.
    -   **Key evidence or reasoning used:**
        -   The "mechanistic gaze" of FR extracts personal features into statistical images that undergo algorithmic treatment.
        -   This is a "reductive engagement" because FR cannot discern the full range of human emotions or personal attributes.
        -   Students might even "game the system" by feigning expressions to be classified as "learning."

-   **Supporting Point 10:**
    -   **Topic Sentences:**
        > Another unsettling reduction of facial recognition technologies is their role in foregrounding fixed attributions of students' race and gender in informing school decision-making. Yet, even if these identifications are rendered more technically accurate, it can be argued that sorting students into socially constructed racialised and/or gendered categories remains a discriminatory practice – conflating biological characteristics with social attributes.
    -   **Key evidence or reasoning used:**
        -   FR disproportionately emphasizes "detecting" the gender and race of individuals.
        -   Controversies have shown some FR systems' inability to discern non-white faces due to racially skewed training data.
        -   Even if technically accurate, sorting students into racialized/gendered categories is discriminatory.
        -   FR technology can "resuscitate long-debunked race 'science'" by attempting to formalize phenotypic differences.
        -   Stark (2019) argues that even in non-racist societies, FR would incline towards racism; in societies that are already racist, FR exacerbates that animus.

-   **Supporting Point 11:**
    -   **Topic Sentences:**
        > Another point of concern is the inescapability of facial monitoring within school contexts. While such coercion applies to the use of facial recognition in all public spaces, it is especially acute in schools.
    -   **Key evidence or reasoning used:**
        -   Facial data is inherently linked to individuals, making them susceptible to constant and permanent surveillance.
        -   Students have no option to self-curate or restrict the facial data they share.
        -   There is no right to decline "non-cooperative" facial recognition systems used for security and attendance.
        -   This coercion is acute in schools due to policies like dress codes that prevent students from covering their faces.
        -   Any promise of "informed consent" is inadequate because security systems require complete sweeps, and the system must scan a face even to recognize an opt-out.

-   **Supporting Point 12:**
    -   **Topic Sentences:**
        > Proponents of facial recognition (and surveillance technology in general) usually counter criticism of compulsory scanning with arguments along the lines of 'if you have nothing to hide then you have nothing to fear'. While questionable in any context, this argument overlooks the value for some students to have an opportunity to hide while in school.
    -   **Key evidence or reasoning used:**
        -   Constant facial recognition significantly curtails students' "right to obscurity."
        -   Students lose the ability to "blend into the background" or "go about their business 'under the radar'."
        -   For certain student groups, these behaviors are legitimate coping strategies for navigating school.
        -   Facial recognition leads to the "normalized elimination of practical obscurity" (Hartzog & Selinger, 2018).
